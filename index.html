About me

Product and UX/UI designer with three years in complex B2B SaaS. I design interfaces with dozens of states, rules and edge cases, work on design systems at code level, and turn business goals into clear user flows together with product managers and engineers. My focus is the logic behind the screen: scenarios, states and every edge case are worked out before anything is drawn – so the team doesn't meet them after release. I work AI-first – I build interactive prototypes in Claude Code on the production component library, so engineers get working code instead of mockups and features are approved in weeks, not months.

process

  1. Logic first, screens second

  2. Prototypes in code

works

A block behaves in a way nobody expects – a temporary limit, a trigger that fires differently – and nothing on the canvas said so until something broke. The tip sits on the block itself and explains it right where the question comes up, and the same card doubles as a short local onboarding: a few steps in a row, with a counter so you see how much is left.

The editor kept quiet – a small thing would break, or there would be an obvious next step, and nobody told the user either way. Now the canvas speaks up: it flags a minor glitch before it costs an hour, or points to what to do next. Several at once stack into one pile instead of burying the screen – unfold it, dismiss it, or switch it off for good.

Newcomers assembled a scenario and stopped short of launching it, and the first run is where it either starts paying off or gets abandoned. The checklist takes them the whole way – from the first trigger to a scenario that actually runs, a real one rather than a demo. Steps tick off by what is on the canvas, not by clicking done, and at the end the whole thing folds into one row.

The whole customer account of a transactional email service, rebuilt in five months. A new customer had to reach their first sent message through sections that had grown up one by one – so I went through all of them: the setup path, every state, a dark theme and mobile. Next to it, a concept for the marketing site with developer docs and an API reference.

A festival pet project, and my favourite thing here. I love Burton – his style, and what sits underneath it – so I built the page like one of his frames rather than a page about a director: a spiral that pulls you in, a sign swinging on its strings, Jack looking down over the title. Made for the pleasure of it.

The part I enjoyed most happened before the layout: briefing, references, competitors, and a long read into who actually buys these cars and why the marque is valued the way it is. The page is what that research turned into – one car taking the whole first screen, and the few facts a buyer really weighs kept quiet underneath it. The dealership is invented.

A workshop that sells furniture without the showroom gloss: a warm photograph, the studio's own story told in lower case, and the name set large enough to work as a sign. Personal concept, invented brand.

A retreat above the clouds. I got caught up in the atmosphere while I was making it, and the whole page is an attempt to hand that feeling over – the air, the quiet, the sense of being far from everyone. One photograph, one line of type and almost no interface, so the page feels like the silence it is offering. Invented brand.

Agents that keep working while you sleep, so the page is dark and calm instead of loud. I deliberately took out everything that makes AI landings look alike – version badges, blinking green dots, capitals, monospaced digits pretending to be a terminal. One typeface, a lot of air and a single promise, so it reads as something that works rather than something being announced. From a blank page to a live site in a day; the product is invented.

Twenty of 250+ responsive templates – gallery templates for the editor and campaign letters for holidays, sales, digests and order flows. Each one is laid out for a desktop and for a phone, where most of them actually get opened.

Two dystopias set in type – Orwell's 1984 and Zamyatin's We. No illustration anywhere: only words, grids and a single red, so the type itself does the shouting and the watching. I made these to let something out – no brief and no client, just the pleasure of saying it loudly.
